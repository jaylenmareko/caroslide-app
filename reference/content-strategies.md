# Content strategies

Three distinct slide-arc structures — pick one per carousel, independent of which visual template gets used. Default: infer from the topic (see below); the user can always just say which one they want instead.

This is the team's actual working vocabulary (from a 2026-07-06 SwipeMaxx planning call, Erick/Matthew/Jaylen) — **"growth" is the working name for the narrative strategy below**, not a fourth option. When a brand's own team talks about "growth vs. value vs. education" content, this is the mapping.

## 1. Value post

Direct, useful, no narrative required — each slide can stand alone. Best for evergreen tips, mistakes-to-avoid, stat-led advice.

| Slide | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 (cover) | States the value on offer — "5 photo mistakes killing your matches," a single sharp stat, or a direct claim |
| 2-4 | One tip / mistake / stat per slide, in any order — no slide depends on the one before it |
| 5 | CTA |

**Real reference pattern (2026-07-08)** — `@shewolfeofwallstreet` (Amanda Wolfe, personal finance, 307K followers — a completely different niche, confirmed via direct visual inspection, not just a text search). Two variants worth copying:
- **Numbered list with a personality aside** — "Things That Cost You Money, But Make You Richer: 1) Dogs — proven to reduce stress, anxiety, cholesterol & cardiovascular disease. (Unless you have to pry a chicken bone from their mouth...)" The joke/aside after the fact is what keeps a plain list from reading dry — the tip is still the point, the humor is just seasoning.
- **Myth-bust Q&A framing** — "What Is an Emergency Fund? A financial safety net for future unexpected expenses. There's a magic number, but it's not the same for everyone... 'Just tell me how much I need?' The right amount depends entirely on YOU. See the calculations!" This is a direct-answer value post that still earns the swipe with a "see the calculations" hook on the closing slide — a value post can use a light hook/reveal mechanic without becoming a narrative arc.
- Also spotted a real-math comparison post on the same account ("1 million seconds = 12 days, 1 billion seconds = 32 years, 1 trillion seconds = 32,000 years") — that's Education-strategy shaped (a real, verifiable comparison as the "surprising fact"), not Value — useful as a reminder that one account can run both strategies, and the tell is whether the slide needs a mechanism/explanation (Education) or just states a fact/tip (Value).

## 2. Narrative-driven ("growth")

A real arc — trend/news hook pulls them in, a story builds tension, then it resolves into the product. Best when there's a genuinely current event/trend to hook on, not just generic advice. Called "growth" content on the team because this is the format built to actually grow reach/following, not just serve existing followers — it works because it doesn't read as an ad ("it's not just like, hey, use [product]... it makes it a little bit more less salesy").

| Slide | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 (cover) | The trend/news hook — something real and current, not manufactured |
| 2 | Story — introduce the tension/problem the trend creates (a relatable situation, not necessarily a named individual) |
| 3 | Stakes — what happens if nothing changes, the turning point |
| 4 | Resolution — ties the story's tension directly to the product as the fix |
| 5 | CTA |

Unlike the value post, these slides build on each other in order — don't shuffle them. A mentor-to-mentee framing also counts as narrative: "I helped a guy who was scared to talk to girls... he showed me an app... I tried it myself" — same story-with-a-turn shape, just a different hook than a news item.

**Real reference pattern (2026-07-08)** — `@offdating` (40.7K followers, dating/relationship-psychology niche, directly adjacent to SwipeMaxx's audience). Their whole grid runs the same formula and it's worth copying directly:
- **Cover gets all the design investment**: bold all-caps headline, one key phrase highlighted in a solid color block (not just colored text — an actual filled rectangle behind it), plus a striking photo — in their case a consistent stylized cutout/rotoscoped image with a white outline stroke, reused across posts as a recognizable motif. Headlines are all the same shape: "Why She [Does X]," "Why [Behavior] Makes Her [Reaction]" — a named psychological pattern, not a generic tip.
- **Every inner slide after the cover is plain text on a flat background** — no photo, no template flourish, just a short paragraph building the story beat (tension → stakes → resolution). This isn't a shortcut; it's deliberate — once the cover earns the swipe, the reader is already committed, so the inner slides just need to deliver the payoff cleanly without competing for attention.
- **Takeaway for this skill**: the cover slide is where template/visual effort should concentrate disproportionately. A narrative carousel with a strong cover and plain-text inner slides can outperform one with mediocre effort spread evenly across all 5 slides.

## 3. Education

Teaches something real — a study finding, a mechanism, a fact the audience didn't know — and lets the brand's product/claim be the natural payoff of understanding it, not a hard sell. This is the "did you know" / "a study found X" carousel format. Best when there's real science, data, or a credible claim behind the product that a value post would flatten into a generic tip and a narrative would over-dramatize.

| Slide | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 (cover) | The surprising fact or finding — "A 2024 study found X," framed to make someone stop scrolling |
| 2-3 | Break down the mechanism/why — the actual explanation, not just a repeated claim. Cite the real source/finding if the brand has one (a founder's credentials, a real study, a documented ingredient property) rather than a vague "studies show" |
| 4 | What this means for the reader — connects the fact directly to the product as the practical application |
| 5 | CTA |

Slides 2-3 build on each other (mechanism needs the fact from slide 1 to land) but slide 4's "so what" doesn't need to be reordered with them. Only use this when the underlying claim is real and defensible — this is the one strategy where getting a fact wrong is a credibility risk, not just a weak hook.

**Real reference patterns (2026-07-08)** — pulled from accounts that run this format well (`@elevatemindhq`, `@technology`), to give this strategy the same grounded texture Value/Growth get from the transcript. The 5-slide table above is the default shape, but these are legitimate real variants of slide 1 and the mechanism slides, not a different strategy:

- **Numbered-list cover** — instead of a single fact, the cover states a count: "5 Things Your Brain Needs Every Day to Function at 100%." Each inner slide then covers one numbered item with its own bold stat-led sub-headline (e.g. "30 Minutes of Movement") plus a short mechanism paragraph underneath (e.g. "Exercise increases BDNF, which supports neuron health and neuroplasticity — low activity is linked to faster cognitive decline"). This is the same fact→mechanism→so-what shape as the standard table, just repeated per numbered item instead of once.
- **"Swipe for more" as an explicit cover CTA** — a literal on-slide prompt ("Swipe → to uncover...") stacked under the headline. Worth using on slide 1 when the cover is list-style, since it tells the reader there's a specific number of things coming, not just "more content."
- **X-vs-Y comparison format** — "Foods That Damage Your Liver" split into a damage column and a protect column, side by side. A valid education cover/mechanism slide when the real content naturally splits into two contrasting categories, not just a single fact.
- **Ranked list with quantified stats** — "Most Hydrating Fruits" with a numbered ranking and a percentage per item. Another valid mechanism-slide variant when the brand's real source is itself a ranking/dataset, not a single finding.
- These accounts lean on a bold neon/glow visual style — that's their aesthetic choice, not a requirement of the strategy. The transferable part is the structure (bold stat-led headline + a real, specific mechanism sentence underneath), not the look.

## Choosing between them

- A hard news item or trend with real momentum (a platform policy change, a viral stat, a cultural moment) → **narrative/growth**. It has a story in it; use it.
- A real study, mechanism, or credible fact the brand can actually back up → **education**. Don't reach for this without a real source — a fabricated-sounding stat is worse than no stat.
- General advice, tips, or a stat that doesn't have a "story" or a "why" attached to it → **value**. Don't force a narrative arc or a fake mechanism onto a fact that doesn't need one.
- If genuinely unsure, default to **value** — it's the lower-risk, always-works option. Narrative and education only pay off when the hook (a real trend, or a real fact) is strong enough to carry the format.

All three strategies still take exactly one CTA slide (`cta.html`, never flipped/altered) as the last slide, and all three still follow the per-project caption color convention from `templates-guide.md`.
